# The Sentence Nobody Rehearses

In "Buzzing Your Speeches," Ira gets an assignment that has nothing to do with the song she's about to sing: over the weekend, buzz all the talking parts. Not the chorus, not the runs — the spoken intro. Her very first line is something every performer says and almost nobody practices: "Welcome, everyone, and thank you so much for—" and then the assignment kicks in before she even finishes the sentence.

It's a small reframe with a big consequence. The moment you're introducing a song is usually the least-rehearsed ten seconds of any performance, because it doesn't feel like the "real" singing yet. But it's still your voice, in front of the same room, and it's often the first thing people hear. Ira's coach treats it exactly like a vocal exercise instead of a throwaway — buzz it, focus on the vowel sounds, don't worry about the consonants, and the reason becomes obvious fast: if you can buzz a sentence loudly and comfortably over music, you can absolutely speak it that way.

What's worth noticing here isn't the buzzing itself — that's a familiar warmup move. It's where she points it. Most people save their vocal technique for the parts of a performance that feel technical and let the talking parts run on autopilot, quieter, thinner, whatever voice happens to show up. This clip is a reminder that the speaking voice isn't separate from the instrument — it's the same breath, the same support, just aimed at plain words instead of a melody. The sentence you say before the song deserves the same preparation as the song.
